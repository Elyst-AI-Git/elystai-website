# Website page presentation architecture brief

*Prepared 29 August 2026. This is a presentation and narrative pass, not a code brief. The live listai.com domain was not reachable, so the current-state observations use the working checkout and the approved Work OS page direction.*

## Core decision

Do not present the site as four brochure pages that happen to share a design system. Present each page as a guided decision for one person:

1. Orient me: what is this, and is it for someone like me?
2. Recognise me: do they understand the problem I am carrying?
3. Show me: what does the work, session, or system actually look like?
4. Reassure me: what is proven, what stays human, and what do I own?
5. Give me a next move: what is the smallest sensible action?

The pages should share the same point of view but have different jobs and visual signatures. Keep the current restrained green, large type, whitespace, dark navigation, and dither mark. Change the storytelling geometry.

### Audience assumption

I am treating “NHR” as the HR/L&D or people leader who sponsors corporate training. If it means another role, only the Training section changes materially.

| Page | Primary reader | Desired first impression |
|---|---|---|
| Home | Founder, owner, COO, or functional decision-maker in a growing business | “They understand why our AI activity has not changed the work, and they can help us start safely.” |
| Services | Decision-maker who already has one costly workflow problem | “I know exactly what happens, what I receive, where risk sits, and how ownership transfers.” |
| Training | HR/L&D sponsor, department head, or leader buying capability for a team | “This is safe to sponsor, relevant to our people, and practical enough to show up in Monday’s work.” |
| About | A skeptical buyer deciding whether to trust two founders with a real workflow | “The people on this page are the people who will do the work and remain accountable.” |
| Work | Buyer looking for evidence before committing | “I can see what changed, what stayed human, and what the numbers do and do not prove.” |

## Shared rules for a modern presentation

- **Show one thing per page.** One workflow, one session replay, one founder responsibility map, or one proof story is stronger than ten generic cards.
- **Use progressive disclosure.** Surface the decision first; let the reader open the detail. Do not make every visitor read the full methodology.
- **Make every section answer “so what?”** Each section should end in a visible output, behaviour, or decision.
- **Separate sponsor value from participant value.** Training buyers need safety, fit, and follow-through; participants need relevance and hands-on practice.
- **Use diagrams as explanations, not decoration.** A simple flow of input → AI work → human gate → outcome is more valuable than another abstract illustration.
- **Label evidence honestly.** Use client proof only with permission. Label fictional, illustrative, estimated, and provider-reported examples visibly.
- **Make motion earn its place.** Scroll-linked state changes, a before/after reveal, or a workflow replay can clarify. Decorative motion will make the pages feel like an AI agency template.
- **Do not overbuild the interaction.** Start with a static version that works as a diagram. Add interaction only if it helps the buyer compare, choose, or understand.

## Home presentation brief

### What must be perceived

Elyst is a practical operating partner, not a tool reseller, generic strategy consultancy, or training-only provider. The company is selective, honest about fit, and able to move from diagnosis to a working system.

### What must be conveyed

“Bring one workflow that is slow, fragile, or stuck. We will tell you whether to fix the process, adopt an existing tool, or build a small system your team can own.”

### Best order and format

1. **Orient — the promise.** Keep the split hero and dither mark, but put three small workflow examples beside the CTA: lead intake, RFQ/quoting, reporting or documents. Format: hero plus compact example rail. Impression: “This may be about my work.”
2. **Recognise — where work leaks.** Convert the current bullet list into a selectable row of six symptoms. A reader clicks or taps one and sees a one-line consequence: copied data, missed follow-up, slow reports, unanswered requests, or a pilot that never stuck. Format: diagnostic symptom selector. Impression: “They understand the operational problem, not just the technology.”
3. **Show — the route from stuck to owned.** Present Audit → Build → Handover as one continuous horizontal or vertical flow. Under each phase show one artefact: workflow map, tested working slice, runbook plus owner. Format: scroll-linked process diagram. Impression: “There is a real method and a defined endpoint.”
4. **Prove — one workflow in motion.** Show a concrete, anonymised flow such as enquiry → structured brief → human review → CRM/action. Use a regional example only as a relevant illustration, not as a claim that Elyst is a real-estate or logistics product company. Format: replayable workflow strip with one human gate. Impression: “I can picture what they would actually build.”
5. **Choose — implementation or capability first.** Give two clear paths: “We have a workflow to fix” and “Our team needs to get better with AI first.” Format: two decision cards, not a generic services grid. Impression: “I do not have to arrive with the perfect brief.”
6. **Reassure — evidence and principles.** Put the permissioned training proof or a labelled sample artefact before the long principles list. Keep “work before tools,” bounded builds, safeguards, and documented handover as short behaviour statements. Format: proof card plus five principles. Impression: “They are credible without pretending to be bigger than they are.”
7. **Act — one workflow CTA.** Ask for the task, owner, tools, and desired change. Keep FAQ collapsed below or beside the CTA. Format: low-friction diagnostic prompt leading to the audit call. Impression: “The next step is specific and low risk.”

### Visual direction

Keep the dither as the homepage signature. The new visual element should be the workflow replay, not more decorative cards. The section rhythm should move from empathy → explanation → proof → choice → action.

## Services presentation brief

### What must be perceived

This is a bounded implementation engagement with clear gates, not an open-ended “AI transformation” promise. Elyst will not build before understanding the workflow, and it will not leave the client with a demo nobody owns.

### What must be conveyed

“One workflow enters the engagement. A tested system, human decision points, documentation, and ownership come out.”

### Best order and format

1. **Orient — show the whole journey immediately.** Keep the current hero, but place a miniature flow under it: problem → audit → build → handover. Format: hero with a live-looking but clearly illustrative workflow line. Impression: “This is a delivery model, not a menu of services.”
2. **Qualify — let the buyer self-select.** Keep the Works when / Does not work when split. Make it interactive only if useful: the reader can identify whether the workflow repeats, has an owner, has accessible data, and has a measurable cost. Format: fit gate or two-column checklist. Impression: “They will protect me from a bad build.”
3. **Make the engagement tangible.** For Audit, Build, and Handover show inputs, work, outputs, and the decision required from the client. Format: three phase panels with a persistent progress line. Impression: “I know what I am buying.”
4. **Show a representative workflow.** Use one example with existing tools: channel or form → structured record → AI preparation → exception/human review → approved action. Format: annotated swimlane or before/after toggle. Impression: “They can fit into our operating reality.”
5. **Make the human gate visible.** Never hide approval, exception handling, access boundaries, or escalation in a paragraph. Format: highlighted decision node in the workflow. Impression: “Automation will not silently make a consequential decision for us.”
6. **Give Handover the emotional climax.** Show the client owning the system: trained users, named owner, runbook, access transfer, limits, and agreed support. Format: ownership handoff diagram or “six months later” scenario. Impression: “We will not become dependent on the agency.”
7. **Explain commercial boundaries.** Put scope, exclusions, milestones, measure, and phased payment near the FAQ, not buried in a proposal promise. Format: compact “how a project stays bounded” strip. Impression: “This will not expand without my agreement.”
8. **Act — submit one workflow.** CTA should request one task and its owner, not “tell us about your AI ambitions.” Impression: “I can start with the messy version.”

### Visual direction

Do not use the same dither hero as the main visual argument. Services should feel like a working system: lines, states, queues, approvals, and handoffs. Use the dither only as a small brand echo if needed.

## Training presentation brief

### The two audiences

The sponsor wants a safe, defensible programme with relevance, logistics, and follow-through. The participant wants to know that the session will not be a generic AI lecture or a public test of their competence.

### What must be perceived

“This is practical, role-aware, and safe to put in front of my team. People will practise on relevant work and leave with something usable.”

### What must be conveyed

“We design the session around your roles, approved tools, real tasks, and operating constraints. Your team learns what to do, what not to do, and what to try next.”

### Best order and format

1. **Orient — sponsor-safe promise.** Lead with a result such as “A session your team can use on Monday,” supported by “built around your roles, tools, and work.” Format: hero plus a small fact line for format, duration, and audience once confirmed. Impression: “I can explain this purchase internally.”
2. **Recognise — the room’s real problems.** Show three participant situations: “I use ChatGPT but start from a blank page,” “we cannot agree what data is safe,” and “we tried a pilot but nobody owns the next step.” Format: three situation cards or a short room storyboard. Impression: “This is about our actual adoption gap.”
3. **Show — what happens in the room.** Replace a generic curriculum list with a pre-session → live practice → debrief → follow-through timeline. Format: four-step session timeline. Impression: “The session has a designed experience, not a slide deck.”
4. **Let the buyer choose the right format.** Present leadership briefing, role-specific workshop, half/full day, and multi-session programme by desired job, not by product name. Format: decision cards with “best when” and “leaves you with.” Impression: “They understand different stakeholders.”
5. **Replay one session.** Use the Aug 18 session if permission and facts are confirmed. Show the room, one exercise, one output, and one participant or sponsor quote. If proof is not ready, use a labelled fictional session example. Format: session replay with artefact preview. Impression: “I can see the experience and the quality bar.”
6. **Make safety visible.** Use a clear Frame → Think with AI → Verify & decide method. Show data boundaries, tool permissions, human review, and what should never be pasted into a public tool. Format: three-stage loop, not a compliance paragraph. Impression: “This will not encourage careless AI use.”
7. **Show what happens after the room.** Name a lightweight follow-up: one manager-owned action, a 7–14 day check, or a before/after confidence and application signal. Do not promise productivity gains without a baseline. Format: small follow-through card. Impression: “The sponsor will see whether the session transferred.”
8. **Act — plan a team session.** Ask for role mix, team size, approved tools, sample work, desired change, and preferred format. Keep the training CTA completely separate from the audit CTA. Impression: “The conversation will be tailored.”

### Visual direction

Training should feel like a room changing, not a course catalogue. Use participant work artefacts, a session timeline, role cards, and an anonymised before/after example. Keep the dither hero if desired, but do not let it carry the entire page.

## About presentation brief

### What must be perceived

The small team is a strength because the founders are close to the work and accountable through handover. It is a weakness only if the page looks unfinished or cannot prove who does what.

### What must be conveyed

“These are the two people who will understand the workflow, make the hard calls, build the system, and hand it over. Here is how they think and where they operate.”

### Best order and format

1. **Orient — name the relationship.** Use the fixed title “Your AI partner,” followed by the audit/build/train promise and the honest two-person capacity constraint. Format: short hero with founder portraits or a quiet human visual. Impression: “This is personal accountability, not an anonymous agency.”
2. **Meet the people early.** Put the two founders immediately after the hero, with real photos, role ownership, verified bios, and LinkedIn links. Format: two full-width horizontal strips with complementary alignment. Impression: “I know who is responsible for my project.”
3. **Explain the operating model.** Show the founder division of labour as a simple map: discovery/adoption/training ↔ technical scoping/implementation/handover. Format: responsibility map. Impression: “The partnership has enough range without pretending to be a large team.”
4. **Show what you will and will not do.** Convert the five principles into paired behaviours: diagnose before building, bounded systems, explicit human review, workflow measures, and honest refusal. Format: “We will / we will not” statements. Impression: “Their judgment is visible.”
5. **Give truthful place and delivery context.** State Kerala base, legal company details, domain email, company LinkedIn, and remote/on-site model once confirmed. Mention GCC focus only at the level Elyst can actually support. Format: compact operating-facts block. Impression: “They are real, reachable, and clear about their footprint.”
6. **Add proof of people.** Use one permissioned training story, client quote, or anonymised artefact. Format: small evidence panel. Impression: “The founders’ claims connect to actual work.”
7. **Act — meet or book.** Use a low-pressure audit CTA after trust has been earned. Impression: “I can speak to the people who will do the work.”

### Visual direction

About should be the human page. Reduce the repeated dither hero and use portraits, direct founder writing, and a clean responsibility map. Avoid a generic mission statement, team-size theatre, or stock photography.

## Optional Work / Case Studies presentation brief

### Decision

Do not add this page to the main navigation until Elyst has at least one permissioned implementation case and one permissioned training story. The current proof block should come first.

### What must be perceived

“I can inspect the method, the evidence boundary, the human decisions, and the handover without being sold a polished success story.”

### Best order and format

1. Case selector by workflow, not a gallery of logos.
2. Baseline and consequence: what was slow, fragile, or expensive.
3. Before/after workflow replay.
4. What Elyst built, including existing tools and exception paths.
5. Human gate and ownership boundary.
6. Measured result with source and caveat.
7. What the case does not prove.
8. Next workflow CTA.

Use a case-study format inspired by [Change by Design’s end-to-end workflow story](https://changebydesign.ai/case-studies/01-always-on-lead-engine), [Stonecut’s production flow](https://stonecut.agency/work/connected-service-website), and [WorkflowMD’s clearly labelled fictional sample](https://workflowmd.io/consultants). The page should feel like a replayable operating lesson, not a trophy cabinet.

## What should change from the current page system

- Home becomes a diagnosis and orientation page.
- Services becomes a workflow replay and ownership page.
- Training becomes a sponsor-safe room transformation page.
- About becomes an accountability page.
- Work becomes an evidence replay page only when proof supports it.
- The repeated dither hero stops being the main visual argument everywhere.
- Long bullet lists become selectors, timelines, diagrams, or artefact previews.
- Generic AI language gives way to named work, named outputs, and named decision points.

If the smarter model takes only one principle from this brief, it should be this: *the visitor should see their own work moving through the page before they are asked to believe Elyst’s claims.*
